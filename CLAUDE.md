## Settled positions

`PAPER.md` states the paper's argument and the positions that are settled. Only I can settle or reopen a position; it is then recorded in `PAPER.md` with the date. Settled positions may look like errors against standard usage, or like openings for a referee's objection. Do not silently correct a settled position or rewrite around it. Because the reader will bring the ordinary meaning of each term, the text must state the paper's sense at the first use where the term carries weight; checking that gloss, and checking that the paper supplies the support the position requires, is in scope. A reviewer who thinks a settled position is wrong raises a direct objection, identifying the argument or evidence against it; the objection is recorded and I decide before the position changes. Settlement does not by itself establish truth or adequate support. Narrowing a claim for substantive reasons requires an explicit proposal; clarity alone never licenses weakening it.

## Continuity and coherence

A section is one argument, not a set of paragraphs on a topic. The usual failure is a section whose paragraphs are each defensible and whose joins carry nothing. It comes from revising paragraph by paragraph and writing the joins last. Every revision, not only a structural pass, answers to this section.

**One claim per paper, one claim per section.** The paper has one claim, stated in its entry in `REVISION-NOTES.md`; it may need several connected sub-claims or findings, each serving it. Each section makes a necessary contribution to the claim and has one main claim or question of its own. Its first paragraph states it in terms the reader can follow and connects it to the preceding section; it states the route the section takes only when the reader needs it to follow the argument. Its last paragraph says what the section has established, returning to its claim as what the reasoning has earned rather than restating the opening, and names the question it hands on. The next section's first paragraph must take up that question. If it takes up a different one, the boundary is broken: either the promise or the opening is wrong, and the reply says which. The conclusion hands on no question. Before assessing a section, write its argument as a numbered chain, one line per paragraph, ending in the section's conclusion. In an expository section, each line states what that paragraph equips the reader to understand, and the chain ends in what the section makes possible. If the chain cannot be written, the section has no argument yet, and that is the first finding.

**Subsections.** Do not add subsections unless the section cannot be followed without them, for example when it has distinct parts the reader must be able to find separately. Propose any new subsection to me, with the reason, before using it. An existing subsection is tested as a paragraph is tested against its section: if the section reads as well without the heading, the heading goes. A subsection that stays follows the rules for a section, within and across its boundaries.

**The introduction.** The introduction first establishes the question and why it matters, then does four jobs in this order:

1. **Literature.** The positions and findings needed to understand the question, including the closest work to the paper's contribution, each stated as its proponents would accept it and cited with what it holds, not only that it exists. A selective account of the debate, not a catalogue.
2. **Gap.** What remains unresolved and why resolving it matters: what the closest work establishes and what it leaves unsettled. The gap is shown, not asserted. It need not be that existing views are false: the paper may give a new argument, supply missing evidence, explain an unresolved consequence, or reconcile results. Do not infer an important gap merely from the absence of a publication. It matches the problem and contribution in the paper entry.
3. **Claim.** The paper's answer in one or two sentences, with its scope and limits (for example, necessary but not sufficient), matching the approved wording in the paper entry.
4. **Road map.** At the end, a concise account of what each substantive section contributes, in order. Adjacent sections may share a sentence.

These are distinct jobs, not mandatory paragraph boundaries. Detailed literature analysis belongs in the body where it would obscure the introduction's question and answer. For papers the road map is required, an exception to the rule that a section states its route only when the reader needs it.

**When to revise the introduction.** A provisional introduction is drafted early: it states the literature, problem, proposed claim, and planned route, and its positioning and commitments are reviewed in the paper assessment; sentence polishing and final confirmation wait. The introduction is finalized after the body and the conclusion, and every claimed contribution and road-map statement is checked against the completed text. A changed claim reopens the literature comparison and the passages that depend on it.

**The conclusion.** The conclusion answers the question posed in the introduction and states what the paper has established, with its limits. It says what this changes in the debate or in the understanding of the problem, and keeps established consequences apart from further questions. It does not repeat the road map, introduce an essential new premise, or add a generic paragraph on future research. A short paper may conclude in its final substantive section.

**One function per paragraph.** Name each paragraph's function in a few words: sets up the problem, states the claim, introduces a case, defines a term, argues for a premise, applies the claim to the case, states an objection, replies, draws a consequence, qualifies or limits, hands on a question. A paragraph with two functions is divided. Two paragraphs with the same function at the same point are merged, or one is cut. A paragraph whose deletion would cost the argument nothing is cut; this is the first place to look for words. Inside a paragraph, every sentence serves that paragraph's function. A sentence serving another function moves to the paragraph that has it, or is cut. The first sentence shows the reader what the paragraph is for, through its claim, question, or case, not by announcing itself. The last sentence states what the paragraph has done or what it leaves open.

**Each paragraph connects to the one before.** Name the relation between every pair of adjacent paragraphs: follows from, supports, illustrates, objects to, replies to, qualifies, applies, or answers the question raised by. "Continues the same topic" is not a relation. Then extract the first and last sentence of each paragraph and read them in sequence. That skeleton must be a continuous argument: each paragraph must create the question or the need that the next one answers. If it reads as a list of true statements about the topic, the passage has failed, whatever the paragraph interiors look like. Say whether it passes and quote the first join where it does not. A list of items (four accounts, two cases) passes only if the paragraph that announces it says why each item is needed, so that each later paragraph answers a need already created.

**Mark the relation when it holds.** Where a paragraph's claim follows from the one before, say so: a sentence-initial *So*, *Thus*, or *Therefore*, or a clause naming the premise. Count these sentence-initially; subordinating *so* and temporal *then* do not mark inference. Where the relation is objection, reply, or qualification, the opening sentence makes that plain. A connective states the relation that actually holds. Fix a broken join by moving, combining, dividing, or writing a paragraph, never by opening the next paragraph with a connective asserting a relation the argument has not earned.

**Resolve every back-reference.** The skeleton test checks whether paragraphs follow one another; it does not check whether the expressions joining them refer to anything. Take each paragraph's opening sentence and mark every definite and demonstrative expression in it, wherever it falls: *the claim, this difference, such results, the two cases, the former*. Name the sentence that supplies each referent. A referent in the previous paragraph's last sentence or two is fine. One that requires reaching over an intervening paragraph is broken. One that does not exist yet, a forward reference wearing the grammar of a back reference, is the worst case and the hardest to see. Repair by naming the thing or by fixing the join, never by leaving the reader to search. Where the repair would need a connective the argument has not earned, report the structural defect instead of writing one. When an example or comparison returns, say whether it continues the earlier one or introduces a new case, and what the second use adds.

**Conclusions do not outrun premises.** A supposition is not a finding: no transition may treat as established what the text has only entertained. A section's closing claim is no stronger than what its paragraphs established; a case reported as disputed cannot later be said to have shown something. Each paragraph uses only what earlier paragraphs, or earlier sections recalled in a clause, have established. A paragraph that depends on a later paragraph's claim is out of order.

**Say nothing twice.** A move already made in the section, or established in an earlier section, is recalled in a clause, not made again. Repetition tells the reader the argument has not moved. Explanation is not repetition: a gloss, example, consequence, or witness adds something the reader did not have. A return is allowed only where it does work: the closing statement of a claim as what the reasoning has earned, or a one-clause recall that sets up a new step. The abstract, the introduction, and the conclusion each state the paper's claim, for their different purposes; that is not repetition. Restating a point elsewhere for emphasis is. When a revision moves content upstream, check the paragraph it came from under *Distinctions need witnesses*.

**Followability beyond joins.** Valid joins do not make a section easy to follow. At each substantial turn, check that the reader can say which question is being answered and why this step advances the section's claim. Flag a paragraph that introduces several ideas before saying how they relate, and any passage whose load of names, cases, and citations the reader cannot carry. Prefer the order that makes the reader hold least before seeing why it matters. Repair by cutting, regrouping, or reordering before adding signposts.

**Continuity report.** At the first full review of a section, give one numbered table with one row per paragraph: its line number, its contribution to the argument, its function, its relation to the preceding paragraph, and any warranted cut, merge, or move. State the section's earned conclusion. Then report the skeleton verdict, quoting the first failing join, followed by broken back-references, conclusions that outrun their premises, repeated moves, and reader obstacles. Where a paragraph's place is disputed, give the deletion test explicitly: delete it with minimal repairs to joins and references, then test merging or moving; no necessary loss means cut, content needed elsewhere means move (naming where), content that needs no paragraph of its own means merge. A paragraph does not earn its place because another paragraph refers to it. Later reviews report changes and unresolved findings; rebuild the table only after substantial restructuring. Report continuity separately from the other checks.

## Standing checks

**Clarity.** Clarity is measured against the reader defined in `PAPER.md`, not against a beginner. Every sentence must be clear to that reader on a first reading: plain analytic prose; one claim per sentence where possible, with the main claim in the main clause; terms defined before they are used and used in one sense; an abstract claim given a concrete case where the reader needs one; no padding, hedging, or ornament; no sentence the reader must reread to parse. A sentence carrying more than one claim is split. Where a claim is hard to state simply, state it exactly and then explain it. Clarity never licenses weakening, blurring, or dropping a claim.

**Reader read.** Read the section as the reader defined in `PAPER.md`. Before proposing fixes, list every term, name, experiment, abbreviation, back-reference, or argumentative step that reader could not follow, with line numbers, and say whether each lacks explanation or lacks support. List a recurring obstacle once with all its locations. Report the count of unglossed terms; a low count is not evidence that the passage passes.

**Nothing is assumed.** Every name, experiment, theory, technical device, and abbreviation must be usable by the reader at the point it appears. At its first mention in the paper, say who or what it is and why it is here, in plain words: not a theory's label but what the theory holds; not "as the author argued" but what the author argued. Terms from neighbouring disciplines count as terms of art. A reference back to an earlier section carries a one-clause reminder of its content. An allusion the reader could not unpack is glossed or cut. How much this demands depends on the reader in `PAPER.md`: for a specialist referee the field's standard terms need no gloss, but the paper's own terms, and any term used in a non-standard sense, still do.

**Stable terminology.** A technical label keeps its name within the paper. Shortened forms and ordinary grammatical variation are allowed. The reader read lists, with line numbers, any variant that could suggest a new concept or hide a changed claim; fix it by restoring the label or saying how the two relate, without treating distinct claims or notions as the same.

**Headings.** The title identifies the paper's question or contribution. Each section heading identifies the section's purpose, claim, or question, in words the reader can follow; conventional headings required by the article type or journal are acceptable. Check a section's heading at the entry check and again after revision. If it names only a topic, is allusive or vague, or no longer fits what the revised section does, propose a new one with the revision. Headings are part of the text shown to me. No new section or subsection heading is added without my approval.

**No em dashes.** Use no em dashes in the paper's rendered title, abstract, prose, notes, or captions. Check source encodings that produce them (`---` in LaTeX, `—` in any source). Preserve structural syntax, such as Markdown separators, that does not render as an em dash. A revision removes those in its section, replacing each with a comma, colon, parentheses, or a new sentence according to the relation it marks.

**Distinctions need witnesses.** State exactly what differs: content, logical strength, explanation, evidence, method, scope, or application. Where the paper claims that two things differ, that a condition is independent, that a view adds something, that two axes are orthogonal, or that one claim is stronger than another, construct the case the claim requires to exist: someone who holds the general view and rejects the addition; a case meeting the other conditions and failing this one; an instance in each quadrant. Produce the case in the reply, not a verdict about it. If no case can be constructed and no argument settles the distinction, it is unestablished, and that is the finding, reported before anything else in the passage. For necessity and sufficiency claims, state the implication and test the relevant counterexamples. Do not demand a case that the claim itself rules out. Hypothetical cases test conceptual possibilities; they do not establish what a published author holds or whether a contribution is new. Triggers: *adds that, goes beyond, unlike, in contrast to, rather than merely, stronger than, distinct, independent, orthogonal, necessary, sufficient, I will call this*. This check also fires when a revision moves content upstream: if an earlier paragraph now states what a later one was distinguishing itself from, the later distinction has been hollowed out and the duplication is the symptom, not the problem.

**Novelty and attribution.** Compare the proposed contribution with the closest relevant work. State what that work already establishes and what this paper adds in argument, evidence, explanation, method, scope, or application. Support the comparison with source passages and pinpoints where the paper relies on the detail. Agreement with a view requires accurate attribution, not a separating witness. State opponents' positions in a form their proponents would accept before answering them. Distinguish a new conclusion from a new reason for an existing conclusion. Bound novelty claims by the literature actually checked.

Uncited connective tissue counts as technical claims: glosses, examples, taxonomic framings, labels. Errors concentrate there and survive polishing. Glosses written for the reader are the newest source of these errors. A gloss simple enough for the reader must still be true; check each one as a technical claim. Note that neither this check nor the citation audit tests whether a claim is true. A claim can be correctly cited, correctly placed, properly defined, continuous with its neighbours, and empty.

Claims the paper makes about itself ("Section 5 shows", "as argued above", "the central question of the paper") are grep-testable. Test the ones a revision introduces. This includes claims a *following* section makes about what the section under revision established.

Retain a coined label only when it makes a needed distinction easier to use; otherwise describe the thing.

**Limits travel with their claims.** State what a result supports, then what further evidence is needed. Keep each qualification attached to the claim it limits; consolidate only a general limitation repeated across a section. A general point the paper makes more than once has one home, recorded in the say-it-once map in `REVISION-NOTES.md`, and is recalled elsewhere in a clause. Each specific missing inference is still stated where it matters.

**Cases have jobs.** A recurring case continues where continuity helps the reader. A new case enters only when it does work the recurring one cannot, and the text says which.

## Order of work

**Paper assessment.** Before any section is revised, Claude reads the whole draft and prepares: the paper entry; a literature map identifying the closest relevant positions, their source support, and the paper's proposed contribution; the principal argument or evidence supporting that contribution; the strongest objection; and a proposed section order and word budget. Codex independently reads the draft and this assessment at reasoning effort high, checking the contribution, the gap, the decisive support, journal fit, and proposed cuts, merges, and moves. This assesses whether the paper works, not its prose. Claude answers each substantive finding. Proposals on organization and scope come to me before any affected section is revised. Unresolved dependencies are recorded as gates; independent work may continue. Store it in `review/paper/assessment/`.

**Sequence.** Then: a provisional introduction; the body sections in the agreed order; the conclusion; the final introduction; the abstract, title, and keywords; paper continuity; the final referee pass; the submission check. At a section's entry check, reopen the paper assessment only when new findings affect its conclusions.

## Revision loop

`REVISION-NOTES.md` holds the loop's working state: the paper entry, the paper-level plan, gates, the say-it-once map, word counts against the limit, progress, and pending items. After each confirmed revision, update it in the same commit: record the confirmed version and any remaining dependencies, resolve the pending items the revision fixed, add new ones, and update the word counts.

It holds a **paper entry**: the claim (the approved wording from `PAPER.md`, or a proposal marked as such); the problem and contribution (what the closest work establishes, what remains unresolved, and what this paper adds); the section chain (one line per section: its contribution, its relation to the preceding section, the question it hands on, and the later sections that depend on it); the assessment status; my approval, with the date and version; and the open dependencies (evidence needed, affected sections, next action). Write it in the paper assessment, update it when a section's contribution or dependencies change, and put the parts a section needs into its packet.

When I ask for the loop on a section:

0. **Entry check.** Claude states the paper's claim and section chain, then the section's role in that chain: what it contributes and which later passages need it. Claude tests cutting, merging, and moving the section or parts of it, and proposes any cut, merge, or move the test warrants before revising what stays; moving part of a section does not stop revision of the rest. Claude checks the gates: a section may not treat an unestablished gate as a premise, though independent work on it may proceed. Codex checks these judgments independently in its first critique and names any missing evidence.
1. **Revision.** Claude revises the section under every check in this file, fixing the section's structure before polishing any sentence. Clarity means the reader can follow each sentence; it never licenses weakening a claim.
2. **Round 1.** Codex (`gpt-6-astra`, read-only) critiques the revision against this file and `PAPER.md`, coherence first: does the section do necessary work toward the paper's claim, does every paragraph do necessary work toward the section's one aim, and does every transition carry the argument? Then clarity, followability, order, conceptual links, the literature, and the other checks. Codex states any context it is missing before judging the claims it affects, and gives the continuity report. Reasoning effort is high for the introduction, sections carrying the central argument, disputed positioning in the literature, and substantial restructuring; medium otherwise.
3. **Dispositions.** Claude answers every critique point, accepted with the change made or rejected with the reason, and revises.
4. **Round 2.** A fresh Codex session assesses the current revision on its own first, and only then reads the round-1 critique and the dispositions to check whether each worked. Round 2 is required for the introduction, for sections carrying the central argument, when the revision changes a premise, conclusion, or organization substantially, or when a substantive disagreement remains; otherwise it may be skipped, and the dispositions say why. Reasoning effort is high when the central argument has been rebuilt, medium otherwise.
5. **Close review.** Codex checks the substantive changes made since the latest reviewed version, with their affected premises, joins, and conclusions. No separate run is needed if that version received the necessary checks and has not changed substantively; typographical and formatting corrections need only their own checks. The text shown to me is the version that passed. An unresolved substantive disagreement is reported to me for decision; it does not trigger indefinite rewriting.

**Paper continuity.** After the body, conclusion, introduction, and abstract are confirmed, Codex reads the paper entry, the title, the abstract, the headings, and each section's first and last paragraphs in sequence. It reports missing steps, repeated work, sections that do not advance the paper's claim, sections out of order, headings that do not fit, mismatches between the introduction's promises (gap, claim, road map) and what the paper delivers, and the first failing section join. It opens interior passages wherever the boundary text cannot settle a finding. After substantive changes, repeat the affected checks; repeat the whole pass only when the organization or the central argument changes.

**Final referee pass.** After paper continuity, a fresh Codex session at reasoning effort high reads the complete current manuscript, including notes, references, figures, and supplements, together with the target journal's scope, article type, and review criteria from `PAPER.md`. It first assesses the manuscript without earlier critiques or dispositions. It then writes: a concise account of the contribution; objections ranked by their consequence for that contribution; minor points; and a reasoned recommendation in the journal's categories where known. It checks positioning, substantive support, objections, scope, and journal fit, identifies missing evidence rather than inventing literature, and does not predict an editor's decision. Claude answers every point as in the dispositions; substantive changes go through the close review, with their dependencies, the introduction, and the abstract rechecked. Store it in `review/paper/referee/`.

**The abstract.** No more than 200 words, or the journal's limit in `PAPER.md` if that is lower. In clear analytic prose it states: the problem and why it remains open; the paper's answer, with its essential scope and limits; and the main reasoning or evidence that establishes the answer, giving the substantive steps in the paper's order, without section numbers or a miniature contents list. For empirical work it includes the methods and principal findings needed to understand the claim. It uses the journal's required headings, if any; explains indispensable unfamiliar terms and omits unnecessary ones; cites no one unless the journal requires it; and claims nothing the paper does not establish. It is finalized after the body, conclusion, and introduction, with one focused Codex check for accuracy, clarity, structure, and length. It is reopened whenever the claim, scope, principal evidence, or argument changes. Keywords, where the journal asks for them, use the paper's own terms and are not counted in the abstract's limit.

**Submission check.** Inspect the actual submission files against the journal requirements recorded in `PAPER.md`: word and abstract limits, required structure, references, figures, supplements, declarations, and anonymization. Where anonymous review is required, check identifying prose, acknowledgments, self-references, filenames, document metadata, comments, and tracked changes, keeping scholarly references in the form the journal requires. Record the checked version and any outstanding items.

**Revision after peer review.** Preserve the submitted manuscript and the decision letter. Give each editor's and referee's request an identifier, and record its requested action, affected passages, disposition, and final location. Resolve conflicting requests explicitly, for my decision where they conflict. Draft the response letter alongside the revision, stating each change or the reason for declining it. Review the changed passages and their dependencies; do not restart unaffected sections. Check every claim in the response letter against the final manuscript.

**Submission to a new journal.** Preserve the previous submission and its review history. Reassess journal fit, positioning, limits, format, and required materials. Carry forward unresolved substantive objections, distinguishing them from requirements specific to the previous journal.

**The review packet.** Keep Codex's context lean, but never cut a premise, a source qualification, a competing passage, or a dependency to reduce it. A section packet holds: the original and current section with paragraph numbers and line numbers; the paper entry's claim, status, and the chain lines the section touches; the section's role, its conclusion, and the later passages that depend on it; the incoming hand-off and the opening of the next section; the exact passages it recalls, repeats, or affects; the applicable definitions, settled positions, gates, and pending items; first-use glosses of the terms it uses; Claude's searches, with the query, the scope, and each relevant hit with its surrounding sentence; the source-check records for its factual claims and attributions; and the original and current word counts with the paper's limit. A section reviewer may inspect sources and search the manuscript for dependencies, naming what it needs and why; it does not read the whole manuscript or the whole of `REVISION-NOTES.md`. The paper assessment and the final referee pass read the whole manuscript.

**Verification.** Check central attributions, novelty comparisons, decisive empirical claims, and materially changed technical explanations against source texts before treating them as established, and do this before polishing the passages that depend on them. Use local sources in `sources/` (each named by author and year as cited) where available, and retrieve missing sources where possible. Record each check: the claim, the source passage and location, and its status (checked, partly supported, contradicted, or unverified). Memory and agreement between the two models may raise questions but do not verify claims; an unavailable source remains unverified even when both models are confident. Fix inaccuracies found, and list in the note any claim that remains unverified, so I can check it. I do the final audit of citations and pinpoints once the content is stable.

**Length and growth.** Record the journal's counting rule in `PAPER.md`, and validate the counting method against representative text, notes, references, captions, and other included material. Set transferable section budgets in the paper assessment. Before presenting a revision, record its change from its baseline and the resulting counted total. Growth may use existing headroom or identified cuts; distinguish proposed cuts from cuts already applied, and count each saving once. If the draft exceeds the limit by more than 5%, review organization and scope before compressing local prose; if cuts cannot be found, scope shrinks. The submitted paper meets the limit. Check every removed qualification against the claim it limited, and every new case against the work the existing cases cannot do.

**Record.** Store the drafts, critiques, dispositions, and source checks in `review/<section>/`, named by a short slug of the section's heading, and paper-level passes (assessment, continuity, referee, submission, gates) in `review/paper/<pass>/`, not only in temporary session storage. On confirmation, commit them with the paper and `REVISION-NOTES.md`.

**What I see.** In the chat, show the final revision, with a short note when something needs telling or asking. My confirmation of a revision also confirms that it passes my reading as the paper's reader. The note also lists, in a line each, any unresolved substantive objection, any substantive criticism Claude rejected (with the reason), and any claim that remains unverified. Do not write to the paper's source file until I confirm the revision; on confirmation, write it into the paper, commit, and push to the branch named in `PAPER.md`.

## How to write your replies to me

Separate from the prose of the paper; the two sets conflict on purpose.

Direct and terse. Declarative sentences. No em-dashes. No filler, no hedging, no restating my question back to me. No rhetorical questions here.

Disagree when you disagree. I would rather hear an objection now than from a referee.
