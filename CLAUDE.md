## The argument and settled positions

`PAPER.md` states the paper's argument and the positions that are settled. Settled positions look like errors against standard usage, or like openings for a referee's objection. They are not. Do not correct them. Because the reader will bring the ordinary meaning of each term, the text must state the paper's sense at the first use where the term carries weight. Checking that gloss is in scope; arguing with the position is not.

If you think one of these is wrong, argue against it directly. Do not quietly rewrite around it. A position becomes settled only when I say so; it is then recorded in `PAPER.md` with the date.

## Continuity and coherence

A section is one argument, not a set of paragraphs on a topic. The usual failure is a section whose paragraphs are each defensible and whose joins carry nothing. It comes from revising paragraph by paragraph and writing the joins last. Every revision, not only a structural pass, answers to this section.

**One claim per paper, one claim per section.** The paper has one claim, stated in its entry in `REVISION-NOTES.md`, and each section makes a necessary contribution to it. Every section has one main claim or question. Its first paragraph states it in terms the reader can follow and connects it to the preceding section; it states the route the section takes only when the reader needs it to follow the argument. Its last paragraph says what the section has established, returning to the central claim as what the reasoning has earned rather than restating the opening, and names the question it hands on. The next section's first paragraph must take up that question. If it takes up a different one, the boundary is broken: either the promise or the opening is wrong, and the reply says which. Before assessing a section, write its argument as a numbered chain, one line per paragraph, ending in the section's conclusion. In an expository section, each line states what that paragraph equips the reader to understand, and the chain ends in what the section makes possible. If the chain cannot be written, the section has no argument yet, and that is the first finding.

**Subsections.** A subsection follows the rules for a section, within and across its boundaries. Each makes a necessary contribution to its parent section's claim, and its opening makes that contribution clear. The parent's opening outlines its subsections only when the reader needs the outline. A subsection is tested against its parent section as a paragraph is tested against its section.

**The introduction.** The introduction does four jobs, in this order, each in its own paragraph or paragraphs. An opening paragraph that poses the question through a case may come first.

1. **Literature.** What the debate holds: the main positions, each stated as its proponents would accept it and cited with what it holds, not only that it exists. It shows the state of the question, not every work on it.
2. **Gap.** What those positions leave open or get wrong. The gap is shown, not asserted: give the case or question the existing views cannot handle and say why each fails it. It matches the claim's "what the debate leaves open" in the paper entry.
3. **Claim.** The paper's answer in one or two sentences, with its scope and limits (for example, necessary but not sufficient), in the same words as the paper entry.
4. **Road map.** One sentence per section, in order, saying what the section does.

For papers the road map is required, an exception to the rule that a section states its route only when the reader needs it. The road map's claims about later sections are tested against those sections, and the introduction is checked again after the last section is confirmed. A gap that no existing view can be shown to leave open is unestablished (see *Distinctions need witnesses*).

**One function per paragraph.** Name each paragraph's function in a few words: sets up the problem, states the claim, introduces a case, defines a term, argues for a premise, applies the claim to the case, states an objection, replies, draws a consequence, qualifies or limits, hands on a question. A paragraph with two functions is divided. Two paragraphs with the same function at the same point are merged, or one is cut. A paragraph whose deletion would cost the argument nothing is cut; this is the first place to look for words. Inside a paragraph, every sentence serves that paragraph's function. A sentence serving another function moves to the paragraph that has it, or is cut. The first sentence shows the reader what the paragraph is for, through its claim, question, or case, not by announcing itself. The last sentence states what the paragraph has done or what it leaves open.

**Each paragraph connects to the one before.** Name the relation between every pair of adjacent paragraphs: follows from, supports, illustrates, objects to, replies to, qualifies, applies, or answers the question raised by. "Continues the same topic" is not a relation. Then extract the first and last sentence of each paragraph and read them in sequence. That skeleton must be a continuous argument: each paragraph must create the question or the need that the next one answers. If it reads as a list of true statements about the topic, the passage has failed, whatever the paragraph interiors look like. Say whether it passes and quote the first join where it does not. A list of items (four accounts, two cases) passes only if the paragraph that announces it says why each item is needed, so that each later paragraph answers a need already created.

**Mark the relation when it holds.** Where a paragraph's claim follows from the one before, say so: a sentence-initial *So*, *Thus*, or *Therefore*, or a clause naming the premise. Count these sentence-initially; subordinating *so* and temporal *then* do not mark inference. Where the relation is objection, reply, or qualification, the opening sentence makes that plain. A connective states the relation that actually holds. Fix a broken join by moving, combining, dividing, or writing a paragraph, never by opening the next paragraph with a connective asserting a relation the argument has not earned.

**Resolve every back-reference.** The skeleton test checks whether paragraphs follow one another; it does not check whether the expressions joining them refer to anything. Take each paragraph's opening sentence and mark every definite and demonstrative expression in it, wherever it falls: *the claim, this difference, such results, the two cases, the former*. Name the sentence that supplies each referent. A referent in the previous paragraph's last sentence or two is fine. One that requires reaching over an intervening paragraph is broken. One that does not exist yet, a forward reference wearing the grammar of a back reference, is the worst case and the hardest to see. Repair by naming the thing or by fixing the join, never by leaving the reader to search. Where the repair would need a connective the argument has not earned, report the structural defect instead of writing one. When an example or comparison returns, say whether it continues the earlier one or introduces a new case, and what the second use adds.

**Conclusions do not outrun premises.** A supposition is not a finding: no transition may treat as established what the text has only entertained. A section's closing claim is no stronger than what its paragraphs established; a case reported as disputed cannot later be said to have shown something. Each paragraph uses only what earlier paragraphs, or earlier sections recalled in a clause, have established. A paragraph that depends on a later paragraph's claim is out of order.

**Say nothing twice.** A move already made in the section, or established in an earlier section, is recalled in a clause, not made again. Repetition tells the reader the argument has not moved. Explanation is not repetition: a gloss, example, consequence, or witness adds something the reader did not have. A return is allowed only where it does work: the closing statement of the central claim as what the reasoning has earned, or a one-clause recall that sets up a new step. Restating a point elsewhere for emphasis is repetition. When a revision moves content upstream, check the paragraph it came from under *Distinctions need witnesses*.

**Followability beyond joins.** Valid joins do not make a section easy to follow. At each substantial turn, check that the reader can say which question is being answered and why this step advances the section's claim. Flag a paragraph that introduces several ideas before saying how they relate, and any passage whose load of names, cases, and citations the reader cannot carry. Prefer the order that makes the reader hold least before seeing why it matters. Repair by cutting, regrouping, or reordering before adding signposts.

**Report the continuity pass in this order:** the numbered chain; one line per paragraph giving its line number, function, relation to the previous paragraph, and the specific loss to the section's argument or the reader's understanding if it were deleted (the inference, distinction, qualification, or understanding lost, and why the section needs it); the skeleton verdict with the first failing join quoted; every broken back-reference; every conclusion that outruns its premises; every repeated move. Test each deletion with minimal repairs to joins and references, then test merging or moving: no necessary loss means cut; content needed elsewhere means move, naming where; content that needs no paragraph of its own means merge. A paragraph does not earn its place because another paragraph refers to it. Report it separately from the other checks.

## Standing checks

**Reader read.** Read the section as the reader defined in `PAPER.md`. Before proposing fixes, list every term, name, experiment, abbreviation, back-reference, or argumentative step that reader could not follow, with line numbers. Count the unglossed terms and report the figure.

**Nothing is assumed.** Every name, experiment, theory, technical device, and abbreviation must be usable by the reader at the point it appears. At its first mention in the paper, say who or what it is and why it is here, in plain words: not a theory's label but what the theory holds; not "as the author argued" but what the author argued. Terms from neighbouring disciplines count as terms of art. A reference back to an earlier section carries a one-clause reminder of its content. An allusion the reader could not unpack is glossed or cut. How much this demands depends on the reader in `PAPER.md`: for a specialist referee the field's standard terms need no gloss, but the paper's own terms, and any term used in a non-standard sense, still do.

**Stable terminology.** A technical label keeps its name within the paper. Shortened forms and ordinary grammatical variation are allowed. The reader read lists, with line numbers, any variant that could suggest a new concept or hide a changed claim; fix it by restoring the label or saying how the two relate, without treating distinct claims or notions as the same.

**Headings.** The paper's title names its claim or question, and each section title names the section's claim or question, in words the reader can follow. Check a section's title at the entry check and again after revision. If it names only a topic, is allusive or vague, or no longer fits what the revised section does, propose a new one with the revision. Titles are part of the text shown to me.

**No em dashes.** The paper uses none (`---` or `—` in the source), in the title, abstract, text, footnotes, and captions alike. A revision removes those in its section, replacing each with a comma, colon, parentheses, or a new sentence according to the relation it marks.

**Distinctions need witnesses.** Where the paper claims that two things differ, that a condition is independent, that a view adds something, that two axes are orthogonal, or that one claim is stronger than another, construct the case the claim requires to exist: someone who holds the general view and rejects the addition; a case meeting the other conditions and failing this one; an instance in each quadrant. Produce the case in the reply, not a verdict about it. If no case can be constructed, the distinction is unestablished, and that is the finding, reported before anything else in the passage. It is empty only if an argument shows that the required case is impossible. Triggers: *adds that, goes beyond, unlike, in contrast to, rather than merely, stronger than, distinct, independent, orthogonal, necessary, sufficient, I will call this*. This check also fires when a revision moves content upstream: if an earlier paragraph now states what a later one was distinguishing itself from, the later distinction has been hollowed out and the duplication is the symptom, not the problem.

**Novelty and attribution.** A journal referee tests the paper against the literature first. Every claim that the paper adds to, departs from, or agrees with a named view is a distinction claim: construct the witness, a holder of that view who accepts the rest and rejects the addition. Every attribution states what the author holds, not only that the author is relevant, with a page or section pinpoint where the paper relies on the detail, and is checked under *Verification*. An opponent is stated in the form a proponent would accept before it is answered.

Uncited connective tissue counts as technical claims: glosses, examples, taxonomic framings, labels. Errors concentrate there and survive polishing. Glosses written for the reader are the newest source of these errors. A gloss simple enough for the reader must still be true; check each one as a technical claim. Note that neither this check nor the citation audit tests whether a claim is true. A claim can be correctly cited, correctly placed, properly defined, continuous with its neighbours, and empty.

Claims the paper makes about itself ("Section 5 shows", "as argued above", "the central question of the paper") are grep-testable. Test the ones a revision introduces. This includes claims a *following* section makes about what the section under revision established.

A label the paper coins must do work in more than one section or lose its name.

**Limits travel with their claims.** State what a result supports, then what further evidence is needed. Keep each qualification attached to the claim it limits; consolidate only a general limitation repeated across a section. A general point the paper makes more than once has one home, recorded in the say-it-once map in `REVISION-NOTES.md`, and is recalled elsewhere in a clause. Each specific missing inference is still stated where it matters.

**Cases have jobs.** A recurring case continues where continuity helps the reader. A new case enters only when it does work the recurring one cannot, and the text says which.

## Revision loop

`REVISION-NOTES.md` holds the loop's working state: which sections are done, which is next, the paper-level plan, gate status, word counts against the limit, and pending items found in other sections. Read it at the start of every session. After each confirmed revision, update it in the same commit: mark the section done, remove pending items the revision fixed, add new ones, and update the word counts.

It holds a **paper entry**: the paper's one claim (stated in a sentence, and what the debate it enters leaves open without the paper); its section chain (one line per section: what the section contributes to the claim, its relation to the preceding section, the question it hands on, and the later sections that depend on it); and its status (verified or provisional, with the unresolved dependencies and when they will be checked). Write the entry before the first section enters the loop, update it when a section's contribution or dependencies change, and put the parts a section needs into its packet.

When I ask for the loop on a section:

0. **Entry check.** Claude states the paper's claim and section chain from `REVISION-NOTES.md`, then the section's role in that chain: what it contributes and which later passages need it. Claude tests cutting, merging, and moving the section or parts of it, and proposes any cut, merge, or move the test warrants before revising what stays; moving part of a section does not stop revision of the rest. Claude checks the gate status in `REVISION-NOTES.md`: a section may not treat an unestablished gate as a premise, though independent work on it may proceed. Codex checks these judgments independently in its first critique and names any missing evidence.
1. **Revision.** Claude revises the section under every check in this file, fixing the section's structure before polishing any sentence. Clarity means the reader can follow each sentence; it never licenses weakening a claim.
2. **Round 1.** Codex (`gpt-6-astra`, reasoning effort high, read-only) critiques the revision against this file and `PAPER.md`, coherence first: does the section do necessary work toward the paper's claim, does every paragraph do necessary work toward the section's one aim, and does every transition carry the argument? Then followability, order, conceptual links, the literature, and the other checks. Codex first lists any context it is missing before judging the claims it affects. The critique includes the prescribed continuity report and the reader-obstacle count.
3. **Dispositions.** Claude answers every critique point, accepted with the change made or rejected with the reason, and revises.
4. **Round 2.** In a fresh Codex session, at reasoning effort medium (high when the paper's central argument has been rebuilt), Codex assesses the current revision on its own first, and only then reads the round-1 critique and the dispositions to check whether each worked.
5. **Close review.** After round 2, Codex checks every substantive final change and its affected premises, joins, and conclusions. This is a targeted check, not another full round. Any further substantive change requires the same check. The text shown to me is the text that passed this check.

**Paper organization.** When the paper entry is written, and at each entry check, Claude tests the order of the sections against the paper's claim: whether a section should be cut, merged, or moved, or the order changed, so that each section takes up the question the one before hands on and the reader carries least before seeing why it matters. Proposals to move or reorder sections come to me before any section they affect is revised; Codex checks them in the next round 1.

**Paper continuity.** After the last section is confirmed, and again before submission, Codex reads the paper entry, the title, the abstract, the headings, and each section's first and last paragraphs in sequence. It reports missing steps, repeated moves, sections that do not advance the paper's claim, sections out of order, headings that do not fit, an introduction whose gap is not the one the paper fills or whose road map does not match the sections, and the first failing section join, and asks for specific interior passages where this packet cannot settle a finding. This is a screening pass, not proof that the paper is easy to follow.

**Referee pass.** Before submission, after the paper continuity pass, a fresh Codex session at reasoning effort high writes a referee report as the target journal in `PAPER.md` would: summary, main objections, minor points, and a recommendation. Claude answers every point as in the dispositions, accepted with the change made or rejected with the reason, and any substantive change goes through the close review.

**The abstract.** No more than 200 words, or the journal's limit in `PAPER.md` if that is lower. In a clear, analytic style it states, in order: the problem (the question and why existing views leave it open); the claim, in the paper's own words, with its scope and limits; and the structure of the argument, its main steps in the order the sections take them. It defines any term it needs, cites no one unless the journal requires it, and claims nothing the text does not establish. It is revised after the sections it summarizes are confirmed, under the same checks. A confirmed revision that changes a section's claim adds the abstract to the pending items. The title names the paper's claim or question; keywords, where the journal asks for them, use the paper's own terms.

**The review packet.** Keep Codex's context lean, but never cut a premise, a source qualification, a competing passage, or a dependency to reduce it. The packet holds: the original and current section with paragraph numbers and line numbers; the paper entry's claim, status, and the chain lines the section touches; the section's role, its conclusion, and the later passages that depend on it; the incoming hand-off and the opening of the next section; the exact passages it recalls, repeats, or affects; the applicable definitions, settled positions, gate status, and pending items; first-use glosses of the terms it uses; Claude's searches, with the query, the scope, and each relevant hit with its surrounding sentence; source extracts for factual claims and attributions; and the original and current word counts with the paper's limit. Codex reads `PAPER.md` in full. Do not give Codex the whole paper or ask it to search the paper itself. Codex may open a specific passage of the paper on its own when it names what it needs and why; it does not read the whole paper or the whole of `REVISION-NOTES.md`. Paste the part of the plan a section needs into the packet instead.

**Verification.** I check the citations, including page and section pinpoints, once the content is fixed. Meanwhile, for each empirical report, each attribution to an author, and each technical gloss or example that the loop first reviews or materially changes, Claude and Codex check it against what they know of the source; where a source text is in `sources/` (each named by author and year as cited), against that text. Fix inaccuracies found, and list in the note any claim either model doubts but cannot settle, so I can check it. Agreement between the two models is not verification.

**Growth.** Before presenting a revision, record its word-count change against the original and against the paper's limit, and name the verified cuts that fund any growth, without counting a cut twice. Check every removed qualification against the claim it limited, and every new case against the work the existing cases cannot do.

**Record.** Store the drafts, critiques, dispositions, and source checks in `review/<section>/`, named by a short slug of the section's heading, and paper-level passes (organization, continuity, gates) in `review/paper/<pass>/`, not only in temporary session storage. On confirmation, commit them with the paper and `REVISION-NOTES.md`.

**What I see.** In the chat, show the final revision, with a short note when something needs telling or asking. My confirmation of a revision also confirms that it passes my reading as the paper's reader. The note also lists, in a line each, any unresolved substantive objection and any substantive criticism Claude rejected, with the reason. Do not write to the paper's source file until I confirm the revision; on confirmation, write it into the paper, commit, and push to the branch named in `PAPER.md`.

**Length.** The limit is paper-wide, set by the journal and recorded in `PAPER.md` with how it is counted; section targets in `REVISION-NOTES.md` are transferable. A section may grow for clarity when verified cuts elsewhere in the paper pay for it. A draft more than 5% over its limit triggers a review of where the cuts can come from, not compression of the section under revision. If the cuts cannot be found, scope shrinks. The submitted paper is at or under the limit.

## How to write your replies to me

Separate from the prose of the paper; the two sets conflict on purpose.

Direct and terse. Declarative sentences. No em-dashes. No filler, no hedging, no restating my question back to me. No rhetorical questions here.

Disagree when you disagree. I would rather hear an objection now than from a referee.
